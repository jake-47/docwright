### The Mondaloy pattern

The single tightest documented professional link between cluster members runs through Mondaloy. Mondaloy is a nickel-based superalloy invented in the mid-1990s by Monica Jacinto (later Reza) at Rocketdyne and Dallis Hardwick at Rockwell Science Center. The name is a portmanteau: "Mon" from Monica, "Dal" from Dallis. Hardwick and Reza put their names into the material itself, which means their names are sitting inside the engines launching America's national security satellites whether anyone acknowledges it or not.[^51][^52]

The strategic problem Mondaloy solved is specific and was longstanding. Oxygen-rich staged-combustion rocket engines are the most efficient propulsion architecture known to science. Russia mastered them in the 1960s. The US could not replicate them for decades, not because of physics or funding but because of metallurgy: the metals strong enough to survive high-pressure gaseous oxygen would catch fire, and the metals that did not burn were too weak to hold the pressure. Mondaloy did both. It allowed the US to build the AR1 engine to replace Russian-made RD-180 engines on United Launch Alliance rockets. Mondaloy is used in approximately 12 components of AR1 (preburner, turbine rotor, turbine housing, ducts, lines, hot gas manifold). Two variants exist: Mondaloy 100 and Mondaloy 200, optimized for different temperature and pressure ranges. It can be cast, forged, and 3D-printed.[^51][^52][^55]

Mondaloy became operationally critical in March 2022, when Russia cut off RD-180 servicing in retaliation for Ukraine sanctions. From that date forward Mondaloy was the single material standing between the United States and a gap in its ability to launch national security satellites.

All three Mondaloy patents had expired by December 2012. From that point forward, Mondaloy has existed only as trade secrets inside human heads. Three people knew how to make it end-to-end:

Three, somewhere in between. Livelsberger had partial access to real classified discussions of exotic propulsion, mixed it with speculation, and his PTSD or TBI contributed to the narrative drift.

The key point is that all three readings strengthen the argument that Amy's work was in a field the US treats as extraordinarily sensitive. If gravitic propulsion is real, she was a target.
