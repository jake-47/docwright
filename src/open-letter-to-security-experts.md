An open letter to the security and free-software community

You run what you build and rely on what you vet, but most of us cannot read the source, judge a governance change, or tell a healthy controversy from a fatal one.
So this is a plea, We are asking you for one specific thing: a clear, maintained, plain-language early-warning guide that tells an ordinary user when a project has turned against them and it is time to leave.

We keep finding out too late.
A distribution we trusted begins routing our desktop searches to a third party for revenue.
A core component is quietly replaced by a sprawling dependency controlled by one vendor, and opting out means abandoning the whole system.
A license shifts, a company is acquired, a store backend goes proprietary, and by the time it is undeniable the cost of leaving has been deliberately raised.
None of these announce themselves as the moment to act, which is the whole problem.

What we are not asking for is a political litmus test.
A project's politics are a poor predictor of whether it will keep faith with its users; good projects hold views their users do not share, and many user-hostile decisions carry no politics at all.
The thing that matters is whether the people and incentives steering a project still treat users' interests as the point.

So we are asking you to publish and keep current a checklist of warning signs, in terms a non-expert can apply.
When control concentrates in a single commercial entity and decisions start being justified by its business model rather than user benefit.
When the software begins collecting data, serving ads, phoning home, or requiring accounts by default, especially with no clean way out.
When you can no longer verify what you are running, because closed pieces creep in, builds stop being reproducible, or the license drifts from open toward source-available or proprietary.
When a modular, swappable design is replaced by a monolith that strips your ability to choose or replace its parts.
When the stated technical mission is subordinated to an outside agenda, whether commercial, personal, or political, and good-faith concerns are met with contempt instead of correction.
When ownership or funding changes so that whoever now steers it profits from the very behaviors users would object to.

And we are asking you to teach us how to read these signs, because one of them alone is rarely enough.
Tell us to look for a pattern across several, and to weigh the project's response to criticism most heavily of all, because a healthy project changes course and a captured one rationalizes.
Tell us above all to prepare our exit before we need it, to prefer projects whose data is exportable, whose formats are standard, and whose code can be forked, so that leaving is cheap rather than ruinous.

The proof that this works is already in our history.
Devuan exists because enough people disagreed with Debian's direction and a fork was possible.
LibreOffice, Rocky, and Alma exist for the same reason: the right to leave, used in time, is the only real check a user has.
Help us recognize the moment to use it.

Signed, a user who would rather jump early than drown late.

Here it is in your framing.

---

To the security and free-software experts we rely on:

Please point out patterns plainly and help the rest of us in a way so we too can see it coming: the slow leftward capture of the software institutions we depend on, and the point at which a captured project should be abandoned.

Projects do not get taken in a single stroke we could rally against.
They get turned the way the Fabians described and the way the long march through the institutions actually operates: each step small enough to look reasonable, each objection painted as paranoia, the names and logos and charters left standing while the substance beneath them drifts to a place the original users would have rejected outright if it had been put to them whole.
By the time the destination is undeniable, the cost of leaving has been quietly raised, and the people who warned early have already been driven out or shouted down.

We have watched it happen.


Ubuntu was the friendly door into Linux, and then it began shipping what we typed on our own machines off to Amazon.
Debian carried the Unix philosophy of small, replaceable parts, and then systemd was forced in over the sustained objections of the people who valued that modularity, until opting out of a single component meant forking the whole distribution, which is precisely what Devuan had to do.

And around those, the signature repeats: codes of conduct that police speech instead of judging code, governance bodies that answer to activists rather than users, maintainers purged for holding the wrong opinions, and mission statements that swap "serve the user" for goals the user never agreed to.
Each move, taken alone, was waved through as too small to be worth a fight.
Together they point in one direction.

Here is what we, the laymen, cannot do for ourselves.
We cannot read a governance change and tell housekeeping from the first slice of the salami.
We cannot tell an ordinary disagreement from the opening stage of capture.
We cannot see who now sits on a steering committee or whose interests a new policy actually serves.
So we find out too late, after we have built our work and our habits on ground that has already moved.

This is what we are asking you to write and to keep current.
A plain-language set of warning signs that a project is being captured or already has been: when its decisions stop being defended by what is good for users and start being defended by a political agenda; when dissent is met with expulsion instead of argument; when the people steering it change and its priorities change with them; when loyalty tests and speech codes appear where there used to be a technical meritocracy; when the stated mission and the actual conduct pull apart.
And a rule for when to leave: not at the first controversy, but when the pattern is unmistakable and the project answers criticism by rationalizing rather than correcting.
Tell us to keep our exit ready before we need it, to favor projects whose data we can carry out and whose code can be forked, so that leaving is a decision and not a disaster.

The proof that this is survivable is already in our own history.
Devuan exists because the right to leave was used in time.
LibreOffice, Rocky, and Alma exist for the same reason.
The fork is the only vote a user truly holds, and a vote is worthless if you cannot see the moment to cast it.
Help us see it.

Signed, the users who would rather leave early than be boiled slowly.
